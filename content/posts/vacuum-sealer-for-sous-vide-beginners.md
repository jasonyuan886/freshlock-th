## What Sous Vide Actually Needs from a Vacuum Sealer

Sous vide (French for "under vacuum") does not require industrial-strength suction. It needs three things:

- **Enough vacuum to remove air pockets** so the bag stays tight against the food
- **A reliable seal that survives hours in warm water** without leaking
- **Bags safe for prolonged submersion** at cooking temperatures (typically 130–185°F / 55–85°C)

Many home cooks assume they need a $300+ chamber sealer to do sous vide properly. They do not. Chamber sealers excel at high-volume kitchens and liquid-rich foods, but for everyday home sous vide, a quality handheld vacuum sealer paired with valve-style zipper bags hits every mark at a fraction of the cost.

## Why Handheld Vacuum Sealers Work for Sous Vide

Handheld sealers work with **embossed valve bags** — reusable zipper bags with a one-way air valve built into the top. You place food inside, zip the bag, place the sealer nozzle over the valve, and press one button. The sealer pulls the air out in 10–30 seconds, the valve closes automatically, and you drop the bag straight into the water bath.

The advantages for sous vide cooks are hard to ignore:

- **No heat bar.** Countertop suction sealers rely on a heated strip to melt the bag shut. If you cook sous vide often, you will burn through those strips and replace bags constantly. Handheld valve bags reseal with a zipper — no melting, no waste.
- **Faster workflow.** Skip the custom bag cutting and edge trimming. Portion, zip, seal, cook.
- **Gentle on delicate proteins.** Fish, shrimp, and scallops do not get crushed the way they can with aggressive edge sealers.
- **Portable.** Bring it to a friend's place, a holiday cookout, or a camper van without hauling a countertop unit.

## Can You Seal Liquids (Marinades, Sauces, Juices) for Sous Vide?

This is the big question for sous vide cooks. Liquids are the arch-nemesis of most vacuum sealers — the suction pulls brine, marinade, or meat juice straight into the pump, which can damage the motor over time.

Two workarounds help:

1. **The "freeze first" trick**: Freeze [marinades](/blog/vacuum-sealing-marinades-guide) or sauces in an ice cube tray, then drop the frozen cube into the bag before sealing.
2. **Use a sealer with a drip cup**: FreshLock includes a **detachable transparent drip cup** (mechanical drip-drain) that catches any liquid pulled toward the nozzle before it reaches the motor. Empty it after sealing juicy or marinated foods, rinse, and you are done.

A drip cup does not replace the freeze-first method for very wet foods, but it is cheap insurance against motor damage and makes sealing marinated steaks or brined chicken dramatically less stressful.

## Bag Choice Matters

Look for **BPA-free embossed valve bags** rated for sous vide temperatures. FreshLock bags are BPA-free and embossed (textured) on one side to allow consistent airflow during evacuation, which prevents the "channel block" that leaves tiny air pockets. They come in small, medium, and large sizes — small for individual portions, large for roasts or whole racks of ribs.

Do not use generic smooth zip-top bags. They have no valve, no embossed channeling, and most are not rated for multi-hour submersion at cooking temperatures.

## Common Sous Vide Sealing Mistakes

- **Overfilling the bag.** Leave at least 2–3 inches of headroom below the valve so juices do not get sucked up.
- **Not drying the zipper.** Any moisture on the zip track can break the seal. Wipe it with a paper towel before closing.
- **Ignoring floatation.** Even a well-evacuated bag can trap a tiny bubble. Use a sous vide weight, a stainless steel clip, or a spoon to keep the bag fully submerged.
- **Sealing over bones.** Sharp bone edges can puncture bags. Wrap exposed bones in a small piece of parchment paper before sealing.
