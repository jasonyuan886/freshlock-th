Vacuum sealing dramatically extends food life — but how much, exactly? The honest answer is "it depends on the food." Some [foods](/blog/best-foods-to-vacuum-seal) last 5× longer in the freezer. Some barely last longer in the fridge. Quoting "5× longer" as a blanket claim is misleading. Here is a realistic breakdown of **how long vacuum-sealed food lasts** in the fridge, freezer, and pantry, plus the factors that actually matter.

All numbers below assume proper sealing technique, correct storage temperature, and food that was fresh when sealed.

---

## Why Vacuum Sealing Extends Life

Three things spoil food: oxygen (oxidation, mold, aerobic bacteria), moisture loss, and temperature. Vacuum sealing primarily attacks the oxygen problem. By removing most of the air (a good handheld hits -50 to -70 kPa; FreshLock hits **-60 kPa**), you:

- Slow mold growth (most molds need oxygen)
- Slow oxidative rancidity in fats (the "off" flavor in old nuts, meat, and oils)
- Stop freezer burn (caused by sublimation of moisture into dry air)
- Prevent odor transfer in the fridge/freezer
- Keep moisture consistent (no drying out in the fridge)

Vacuum sealing does **not** kill bacteria (including the dangerous anaerobic kind like botulism in low-acid, moist, room-temperature foods). It does not pasteurize. It does not make spoiled food good. Always use proper temperature control and trust your senses.

## Shelf Life Tables

### Raw meat (fridge 34–38°F / 1–3°C, freezer 0°F / -18°C)

| Meat | Raw, regular fridge | Raw, sealed fridge | Raw, sealed freezer |
|------|--------------------|--------------------|---------------------|
| Ground meat (beef, pork, turkey) | 1–2 days | 5–7 days | 8–12 months |
| Steaks / chops | 3–4 days | 7–10 days | 1–2 years |
| Whole chicken / turkey | 1–2 days | 5–7 days | 1–2 years |
| Chicken pieces | 1–2 days | 5–7 days | 9–12 months |
| Bacon / cured meats | 1 week (opened) | 2–3 weeks | 2–3 months |
| Hot dogs / deli meats (opened) | 3–5 days | 2–3 weeks | 1–2 months |
| Fatty [fish](/blog/vacuum-sealer-for-fish-and-seafood-storage) (salmon, mackerel) | 1–2 days | 3–4 days | 6–9 months |
| Lean fish (cod, halibut, tilapia) | 1–2 days | 3–5 days | 8–12 months |
| Shrimp / scallops | 1–2 days | 3–5 days | 6–9 months |

### Cooked meat and leftovers

| Food | Regular fridge | Sealed fridge | Sealed freezer |
|------|---------------|--------------|---------------|
| Cooked beef/pork | 3–4 days | 7–10 days | 2–4 months |
| Cooked poultry | 3–4 days | 6–8 days | 3–4 months |
| Soups and stews | 3–4 days | 7–10 days | 4–6 months |
| Casseroles / lasagna | 3–5 days | 8–12 days | 3–4 months |
| Pizza slices | 3–4 days | 5–7 days | 1–2 months |
| Cooked rice / pasta | 3–4 days | 7–10 days | 3–6 months |

### Breads and baked goods (room temp or freezer)

| Food | Counter (regular) | Sealed counter | Sealed freezer |
|------|------------------|---------------|---------------|
| Bread (sliced loaf) | 3–5 days | 7–10 days (partially sealed) | 6–8 months |
| Bagels | 2–3 days | 5–7 days | 6–8 months |

Note: full vacuum crushes soft bread. Either partially seal (stop suction early) or use a canister.

## Factors That Shorten Sealed Life

Even properly sealed food does not hit these numbers if:

- **Food was not fresh when sealed.** If you seal 3-day-old chicken, sealing it does not reset the clock.
- **Seal is weak or broken.** A tiny pinhole or loose zip track is all it takes. Check bags after 24 hours in the freezer — any bags that have "puffed up" or feel loose have a bad seal.
- **Storage temperature is too warm.** A fridge running at 45°F/7°C will not give the same life as one at 36°F/2°C.
- **Moisture on the surface.** Pat food dry before sealing. Ice crystals and moisture encourage microbial growth.
- **Food was warm when sealed.** Always cool to fridge temp before sealing.
- **Repeated opening.** Each time you open and reseal, you introduce fresh oxygen.
- **Salt/sugar/acid.** These can degrade plastic bags over very long storage times. Use thicker bags for long-term freezer storage.

## Foods That Should NOT Be Stored Vacuum-Sealed (or Need Special Care)

- **Raw mushrooms** — continue to respire and can build up botulism-friendly conditions if sealed raw. Cook before sealing.
- **Soft cheeses with active mold (brie, camembert)** — only for short periods; partial seal, not full vacuum.
- **Bananas and whole apples** — emit ethylene gas that breaks the seal and speeds ripening.
- **Cruciferous vegetables raw** (broccoli, cabbage, Brussels sprouts, kale, cauliflower) — emit gases that break seals. Blanch first.
- **Garlic and onions raw** — risk of botulism in anaerobic, room-temperature, low-acid environments. Refrigerate sealed garlic/onions, or preserve in oil/acid.
- **Freshly baked bread** — as mentioned, full vacuum crushes it.
