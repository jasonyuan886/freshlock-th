## Why Vegetables Get Ruined in the Freezer

Three things ruin frozen vegetables:

1. **Freezer burn** — Oxygen in the bag oxidizes the surface, causing dry, grayish patches and off flavors.
2. **Ice crystal damage** — Temperature swings in the freezer cause moisture to migrate out of the vegetables and form large ice crystals inside the bag, which rupture cell walls and turn veggies mushy when thawed.
3. **Enzyme activity** — Raw vegetables contain enzymes that, even in the freezer, slowly break down color, texture, and flavor over months.

[Vacuum sealing](/blog/vacuum-sealer-for-fish-and-seafood-storage) fixes #1 and #2 directly. Blanching fixes #3. Together, they are the one-two punch of serious vegetable preservation.

## The Step Before Sealing: Blanching

You must blanch most vegetables before vacuum sealing and freezing. Blanching is the simple process of briefly boiling vegetables and then plunging them into ice water to stop the cooking. It:

- **Denatures the enzymes** that cause flavor, color, and texture loss
- **Sets the color** so green beans and broccoli stay bright
- **Wilts greens** so they take up less space
- **Cleans the surface** of dirt and organisms

### Quick blanching guide (boiling water, then ice bath):

- Green beans, snap peas, asparagus: 2–3 minutes
- Broccoli, cauliflower florets: 3 minutes
- Carrots (sliced), corn on the cob (small): 3–5 minutes
- Spinach, kale, chard: 1–2 minutes (or until just wilted)
- Bell peppers: 2–3 minutes
- Zucchini, summer squash: 2–3 minutes
- Peas: 1.5 minutes
- Corn kernels (off cob): 1 minute

After boiling, drain and plunge immediately into ice water for the same amount of time the vegetable boiled. Drain **very well** and pat dry with towels or a salad spinner. **Excess surface moisture is your enemy** in vacuum-sealed frozen food — it turns into ice crystals.

## How to Vacuum Seal Vegetables for the Freezer

You have two good options: **dry pack** and **tray freeze**.

### Option 1: Dry pack (simple)
Portion blanched, dried vegetables into meal-sized servings. Place in a valve bag, zip, and seal with a handheld sealer like FreshLock. Freeze flat.

Best for: beans, peas, corn, chopped carrots, roasted veggies.

### Option 2: Tray Freeze (individual pieces)
Spread blanched, dried vegetables in a single layer on a parchment-lined baking sheet. Freeze 1–2 hours until solid. Then transfer to vacuum bags, seal, and return to freezer. The pieces stay loose, so you can pour out exactly what you need without thawing a block.

Best for: broccoli florets, cauliflower, mixed vegetables, berries (if you are doing fruit too), diced peppers.

### Technique tips

- **Do not overfill.** Leave 2–3 inches of headroom so bits of vegetable do not get pulled into the valve.
- **Don't crush delicate veggies.** Stop suction when the bag is reasonably tight — there is no prize for turning broccoli into puree. FreshLock's **-60 kPa** suction is strong but controllable.
- **Double-seal if unsure.** If the bag has sharp bits (corn cob ends, cut carrot edges), you can use a double bag or wrap sharp edges in a small piece of parchment.
- **Label the bag with the vegetable, date, and portion size.** You will not remember in December which anonymous green block you sealed in July.

## Vegetables That Don't Need Blanching

A few vegetables freeze well raw, or are typically cooked before freezing:

- **Onions and peppers** (for cooking) — chop, seal, freeze. They soften when thawed anyway, so texture loss is not an issue.
- **Herbs** — freeze in ice cube trays with olive oil, then seal the cubes.
- **Tomatoes** — best frozen raw and slipped out of their skins after thawing, or cook into sauce first.
- **Mushrooms** — sauté first in a little butter, cool, then seal and freeze. Raw mushrooms tend to get mushy.

---

## Vegetables to Avoid Sealing Raw in Bags

- **Whole cabbage heads, Brussels sprouts on the stalk** — they release gas and can break the seal. Blanch or chop first.
- **Lettuce, cucumber, celery, radishes** — super-high water content veggies that turn mushy when frozen regardless of packaging. Best eaten fresh, or pickle them.
- **Raw potatoes** — blanch or cook first. Raw potatoes turn black and grainy in the freezer.

## Thawing Frozen Sealed Vegetables

Most of the time, **do not thaw them**. Drop frozen sealed vegetables directly into:

- Boiling water (30–60 seconds for green beans, peas)
- Stir fries and sauté pans (toss straight from frozen)
- Soups and stews
- Roasting sheets (toss with oil, roast from frozen)
- Microwave steamer (cut a corner of the bag to vent, or transfer to a covered dish)

Thawing vegetables causes them to release water and get mushy. Cooking from frozen preserves texture.
